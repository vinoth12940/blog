A 24B-Parameter AI Model That Runs on Your Laptop — and the $10M Catch

October 14, 2026 · On-Device AI / Open Models · 9 min read

One 15 GB model file, three classes of hardware, zero cloud dependency.

On October 13, Liquid AI released LFM2.5-24B-A2B — and I think it's the most interesting model release of the month, precisely because it's not trying to win the frontier pissing contest. It's a 24-billion-parameter reasoning model with only 2.45 billion active parameters, engineered for a single purpose: running AI agents on the device in your hand or on the desk under it.

The model is the largest and final entry in the LFM2.5 family, which now spans from a 230M miniature all the way up to this 24B flagship. Base and post-trained checkpoints are live on Hugging Face, and the ecosystem moved absurdly fast — official GGUF, MLX, ONNX, and FP8 builds were all available on day one. Within 24 hours the official GGUF repo had racked up roughly 18,000 downloads, and Unsloth, MLX variants, and community fine-tunes were already proliferating.

But there's a catch sitting in the license file, and most coverage will skip it: free commercial use ends at $10 million in annual revenue. More on that — and whether it should change your decision — at the end.

The Numbers That Matter

  • Total parameters: 24.0B — active per token: 2.45B (MoE, 64 experts, top-4 routing)
  • Architecture: 40 layers — 30 double-gated short convolution blocks + 10 grouped-query attention blocks
  • Context window: 131,072 tokens (4× the predecessor's 32K)
  • Vocabulary: 128,000 tokens, doubled via in-place tokenizer expansion for better non-Latin text
  • Training budget: ~36 trillion tokens (up from ~17T pre-training in the predecessor, plus mid-training at 32K and 131K context)
  • Languages: 16, including English, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Hindi, Spanish, German, Arabic
  • Edge decode speed: 184 tok/s on Apple M5 Max, 114 tok/s on AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 (llama.cpp, Q4_K_M quantization)
  • Memory footprint at the edge: ~15 GB on the M5 Max — the lowest of all compared models
  • GPU throughput: 21,000+ output tokens/sec at concurrency 1,024 on a single NVIDIA B300 (SGLang, BF16)
  • Behavior: reasoning-only — every response starts with an explicit thinking phase

That active-parameter number is the whole ballgame. A dense 24B model charges you for every parameter on every token. A sparse mixture-of-experts with 2.45B active charges you for roughly a tenth of that — which is why it decodes at chatbot-smooth speeds on a laptop fanless-mode battery instead of a datacenter GPU.

An Honest Scorecard

Liquid AI benchmarked LFM2.5-24B-A2B against three larger-spirited peers — Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, and Nemotron-3.5-Lightning (30B-A3B) — across 16 benchmarks, all models running on vLLM. Read the results with the standard caveat: these are vendor-reported numbers. But the shape of the results is what tells the story.

Wins where it was aimed. Loses where it wasn't. That's a feature of honest engineering.

Where it wins — and it's not close:

  • AutomationBench: 27.69 — more than 3× the next-best model (Qwen3.6 at 8.72, Gemma-4 at 5.46, Nemotron at 2.61). This benchmark measures multi-step agentic task automation, and the gap here is the release's headline.
  • ToolSandbox: 80.71 — leads on realistic tool-use scenarios
  • LiveClaw: 48.09 vs 38.19 for the next model
  • BFCLv4: 65.01 — essentially tied with Qwen3.6's 65.00 at the top
  • Instruction following: clean sweep — IFEval 96.75, IFBench 74.69, Multi-IF 84.38, all three leading the group

Where it loses:

  • SWE-Bench Verified: 51.00 vs Qwen3.6's 66.20 — real-world code fixing is a 15-point gap
  • GPQA Diamond: 74.42 vs 83.69 — PhD-level science knowledge favors the bigger active parameter count
  • WebArena Verified: 26.75 vs Qwen3.6's 37.62 — long-horizon web navigation still favors the peer
  • PinchBench: 83.18 vs 89.93 — the one agentic benchmark it dropped
The pattern is legible: this model was post-trained to read context, pick tools, and execute multi-step agent loops — not to win trivia or refactor your monorepo. Liquid AI's own guidance says as much: pair it with retrieval or web search for knowledge-heavy work, because tool-calling is its strength.

One credibility note in the model card that I appreciated: the team acknowledges that patterns from public research benchmarks appear in training data, says it tightened its sandboxes in response, and states its reported results come from sealed evaluation environments. After the benchmark-integrity mess this industry has been through, writing that down explicitly is the right move.

Why the Architecture Is Actually the Story

Most MoE releases are transformer-plus-routing. Liquid AI's LFM line keeps a foot in a different camp: a hybrid of convolution and attention. Of the 40 layers, 30 are double-gated short convolution blocks — cheap, local, constant-memory sequence mixers — and only 10 are grouped-query attention blocks. Convolution handles the local pattern work at a fraction of the memory cost; attention steps in where global context actually matters.

Combine that with top-4-of-64 expert routing and you get the economics: transformer-quality output with a per-token compute bill closer to a 2B model. The 131K context growth and the doubled 128K vocabulary (via in-place tokenizer expansion, which improves non-Latin token density) make it usable as a real agent brain rather than a demo.

The deployment story is unusually mature for a day-one release. Official checkpoints ship as native safetensors, FP8, GGUF (BF16, F16, Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and a QAD-Q4_0 variant), ONNX, and MLX at 4/5/8-bit. The model card documents support in llama.cpp, MLX, vLLM, SGLang, and ONNX runtimes, with recommended generation settings of temperature 0.1, top-k 50, and a 1.05 repetition penalty. Any OpenAI-compatible local backend can serve it, and the card explicitly lists agent harnesses — including Hermes Agent, OpenClaw, and Pi — as integration targets.

The Real Product: A Privacy Default

Here's why I think this release matters more than its benchmark sheet. The interesting question in agent design for the past year hasn't been "can a model use tools" — it's been "where does the data go when it does." Every agent that ships your email, calendar, health data, or message history to a cloud API makes a privacy promise it can't technically keep.

The hybrid pattern: the small local brain does the reading and routing; only the genuinely hard reasoning crosses the network.

A 2B-active model at 184 tok/s changes the default configuration of a personal agent. The reading, the routing, the tool-call formulation, the multi-step loops over your actual data — all of it can happen in RAM on the device that already holds the data. The cloud call becomes the exception you route to deliberately, carrying a sanitized query instead of your whole context window.

I've written before about giving agents ears and eyes on a headless Linux box, and the constant tension in those builds is capability versus data locality — every capability upgrade historically meant shipping more raw context to someone else's GPU. A model that scores 96.75 on IFEval and runs at real-time speeds on the same box it's monitoring collapses that tension for a large class of agent work.

Privacy stops being a pricing tier when the model comes to the data. That's the quiet thesis of this release.

For anyone building on-device products — iOS assistants, offline-first tools, embedded agents — this is the first open-weight release I've seen where the agentic capability and the edge economics arrived in the same package. GPT-class capability in a 15 GB file you can hold.

The $10M Catch

Now the part the launch posts won't emphasize. LFM2.5-24B-A2B is open-weight but not open-license. It ships under the LFM Open License v1.0, and Section 5 draws a line in the sand:

Open weights, capped terms. Your fine-tune inherits the license — quantizing doesn't launder it.

  • Below $10M annual revenue: full commercial use is free. Ship products, fine-tune, redistribute.
  • Above $10M: commercial use is explicitly not licensed under this agreement — you're in enterprise-negotiation territory with Liquid AI.
  • Derivatives inherit the terms — your fine-tune doesn't escape the cap.
  • The community repos aren't a loophole: the official and community GGUF, MLX, and ONNX uploads carry the same license file.
  • Non-profits and research: always free, no threshold.

If this structure feels familiar, it should — it's the same cap-and-negotiate pattern that's become the standard fiscal design for open-weight releases: free enough to build an ecosystem, capped so the lab captures enterprise value. For an indie developer or a seed-stage startup, it functionally is an MIT license. For a scale-up, it's a ticking integration decision: build on it now, budget for a licensing negotiation or a migration later.

Should You Build on It?

My take after pulling apart the release:

  • Build on it if you're shipping an agent whose value lives in tool-calling discipline over local or private data — personal assistants, home-server automation, offline-first mobile products, privacy-positioned apps. The AutomationBench gap plus the edge speeds plus the 131K context is a genuinely new point on the capability-per-watt curve.
  • Don't build on it if your core loop is large-repo code generation (the SWE-Bench gap is real) or knowledge-heavy reasoning without retrieval (the GPQA gap, and the vendor itself recommends pairing with search).
  • Verify before you bet the roadmap: these are vendor-run benchmarks. The sealed-environment claim is a good sign, but I'd want independent replications of the AutomationBench number before treating 3× as settled fact — the history of this industry says wait for the third-party reruns.
  • Read Section 5 of the license before your pitch deck cites "open-weight" as a moat. Under $10M revenue, it's a non-issue. Above it, it's your next procurement conversation.

The model I run my own automation on is a 2B-active MoE from a different lab, and I watched that class of model go from toy to workhorse in about a year. LFM2.5-24B-A2B is the strongest evidence yet that on-device agents aren't the niche anymore — they're where the ratio of capability to constraint finally makes sense. The $10M line tells you Liquid AI knows it, too.
