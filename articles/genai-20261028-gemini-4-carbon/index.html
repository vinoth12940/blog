Gemini 4 Carbon: Google Gave Its Best Model to Cyber Defenders First

October 28, 2026 · AI / Security / DevOps · 9 min read

Fourteen days from Argon to Carbon. The new model is real — the API is not, yet.

Last week I wrote about PHP2Shell — the decade-old PHP memory flaws that frontier AI models turned from dormant bug into global emergency within days. The system that did much of that work, Wiz's autonomous vulnerability-research pipeline, ran on Gemini 4 Argon, which at the time was Google's newest and most capable model. That was two weeks ago.

Today Google replaced it. Gemini 4 Carbon is the new frontier: number one on Artificial Analysis' freshly updated Intelligence Index v5.0 at 59, state-of-the-art across the full cybersecurity lifecycle — finding vulnerabilities, validating exploits, writing patches — and explicitly built for agent runs that last days, not minutes.

And here's the twist that makes this release more interesting than another benchmark crown: if you're a developer, you're last in line. Not second. Last.

"Today, we're announcing the next frontier model: Gemini 4 Carbon, our strongest model with state-of-the-art cybersecurity and long horizon capabilities." — Koray Kavukcuoglu, SVP of Google DeepMind and Chief AI Architect

What Carbon Is

Carbon is the second Gemini 4 model, arriving exactly two weeks after Argon reached the Gemini app. It tops the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5.0 at 59 — ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 at 58, GPT-6 Astra at 57, its own predecessor Argon at 50, and Claude Fable 5.1 at 49. Look closely at that ordering: one point separates the top three models on the planet. The benchmark race has never been this tight, and that context matters for everything below.

A new number one — by a single point over Opus 5.5.

What Google says Carbon is actually for matters more than the composite score. It is designed for "much harder, long-horizon engineering problems" that demand persistent agentic workflows. Google's internal teams ran Carbon for multiple days on single tasks — root-causing subtle bugs across large codebases, working through low-level systems code, hitting dead ends and backing out of them on its own. That's a different workload profile from "answer a chat" or even "fix one ticket." The infrastructure implications alone are worth sitting with: multi-day autonomous runs mean checkpointing, state management, and budget enforcement become first-class product concerns, not nice-to-haves.

Elsewhere in the table, Carbon jumped +89 Elo to 1,769 on WebDev Arena — good for fourth place, which tells you web development remains a crowd at the top. And per Artificial Analysis' own independent read, Carbon's index lead at its highest reasoning tier comes mainly from complex reasoning, knowledge retrieval, and scientific tasks — it took first place in four of the index's nine individual evaluations. Broad competence, not universal dominance.

  • #1 on AA Intelligence Index v5.0 — 59 points, up from Argon's 50 two weeks ago
  • First on every row of Google's published table except two — FrontierSWE 2 and the OSWorld-2.1 offline subset
  • Security sweep — 88.2% SEC-bench Pro (finding), 54.8% ExploitGym (exploitation), 71.0% CWE-bench (patching)
  • Long context — 96.2% on GraphWalks at 256K–1M tokens, vs 84.2% for Argon; Carbon's own context window is undisclosed (Argon shipped with 1,048,576 tokens)

The Security Story Is the Product

Most labs bolt a security section onto a general model announcement. Google built this one around it, and the benchmarks span the full defense lifecycle rather than a single win.

Find, validate, patch — plus the reliability stats nobody else leads with.

SEC-bench Pro at 88.2% for finding vulnerabilities. ExploitGym at 54.8% for proving they're real. CWE-bench at 71.0% for generating the patch. Google also reports a strong uplift on internal benchmarks for generating secure code, not just working code. Those are the headline capabilities, and they're why the access story below exists — a model that can find, prove, and fix vulnerabilities at frontier level is a dual-use object by definition.

The sleeper hit of the announcement, though, is the reliability panel. Carbon cuts unsupported claims roughly 2x versus Argon — it puts methodological caveats directly into its deliverables or withholds blocked actions with a clear list of what's missing, instead of fabricating around gaps. It reduced overreach by 26% and under-service by 29%, which is the difference between an agent that finishes a multi-step plan inside scope and one that either wanders off or gives up. Anyone who has watched an agent confidently invent an API that doesn't exist knows exactly which numbers those are.

Then there's the prompt-injection number. On Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark, Carbon dropped the attack success rate from 0.7% with Argon to 0.0% — every tested manipulation attempt blocked.

"That doesn't mean prompt injection is solved, but the test appears to be saturated." — the-decoder, on Carbon's Gray Swan result

That caveat is the right one, and I'd go slightly further: a saturated benchmark is a floor, not a promise. Treat 0.0% as "passed every attack we thought of," not "unbreakable." If Carbon lands in your security tooling, you'll still want injection tests in your own harness — because attackers don't run Google's benchmark suite.

The Gate: Why You Can't Call It Yet

The first frontier model to ship through security gates before product surfaces.

Here's the part I keep turning over. Google is not doing the usual "available to everyone in the API today" launch. Because the security capabilities cut both ways, Carbon goes through the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release model testing process before public access, and the first real users are a vetted subset of cyber defenders — starting this week — before access widens gradually through Google's Fairwind Program for trusted partners. Developers, businesses, and consumers follow "as soon as possible," with no date attached.

"We're taking a deliberate approach to rollout and are actively engaged in the U.S. government's voluntary process for pre-release model testing. Starting this week, we will make Carbon available to a subset of cyber defenders and will gradually phase access to additional trusted partners through our Fairwind Program." — Google DeepMind

Two things about that. First, the defender-first ordering is genuinely novel — I can't remember a frontier model where the security community got first movers and the developer ecosystem got told to wait. It's also quietly shrewd: the people most likely to find the model's offensive edges are the ones handed it first, under vetting.

Second, for the self-hosting crowd: there's nothing to self-host. No open weights, no quantized variants, Google-hosted only. This model will never run on your homelab box — the closest you'll get is its API.

When you do get access, the pricing is the same introductory rate as Argon: $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output tokens, with cached input at 95% off — rising to $4/$20 after the introductory window, per 9to5Google. That cached-input number is the one to plan around: 95% off is Google pricing explicitly for long-horizon agent workloads where you keep enormous context warm across hours or days.

Where It Actually Loses

Index #1 is not "best at your job." Google's own table says so.

Now the honest part, which the composite score buries. Carbon's benchmark losses are as informative as its wins:

  • FrontierSWE 2 — the hardest agentic-coding test in the table: GPT-6 Astra wins at 65.5%, Opus 5.5 takes 62.3%, Carbon lands at 61.2%. When the tasks get genuinely hard, Google isn't first.
  • OSWorld-2.1 — Opus 5.5 is the clear computer-use leader at 87.2% versus Carbon's 81.5%. Anthropic's model remains the one to beat at driving a real desktop.
  • Agentic coding overall — on Artificial Analysis' Coding Agent Index, Carbon jumped from 53 to 59 but still trails Claude Code with Opus 5.5 by three points. The thing developers actually do most: Anthropic still owns it.

So the honest one-liner: Carbon is the best generalist with a security specialty; Opus 5.5 still owns pure agentic coding; GPT-6 Astra wins the hardest software tasks. If your daily work is code-first, today's ranking changes nothing about your stack. If your work is security operations, long-running autonomous tasks, or reasoning-heavy research, this is now the model to watch — and for security teams specifically, it may be the most capable defensive tool any lab has shipped.

The Real Signal: a Two-Week Frontier

Zoom out from the scores and the cadence is the actual story. Google shipped two frontier models in fourteen days, and Sundar Pichai framed the speed itself as the message.

"We're seeing significant improvements in long-horizon tasks even compared to Argon, and we've strengthened frontier safeguards." — Sundar Pichai

Google's stated reason for the pace: researchers and models "working hand in hand." Strip the phrasing and it says the quiet part out loud — frontier AI is now substantially built by frontier AI. The same acceleration I wrote about on the offense side last week — Wiz turning a decade-dormant bug into a working exploit in days — is now showing up on the defense side, compressed into a two-week release cycle. Offense got cheaper, and now defense iterates on offense's clock.

Three consequences for anyone building on these models:

  • "Wait for the next model" is now a two-week strategy. Whatever you defer, something meaningfully better arrives before your sprint ends. But with one point between the top three, waiting for a crown is a coin flip.
  • Model-specific infrastructure depreciates fast. Hard-coding prompts, parsers, or routing to one vendor's quirks has a two-week half-life at the frontier.
  • Evaluations must be continuous. A quarterly model bake-off is meaningless when the leaderboard re-shuffles monthly. Whatever evals you run, run them on every release — including the ones your current vendor ships.

What I'm Doing While I Wait

I can't call this API today, and neither can you. Here's what's actually actionable in the meantime:

  • Keep your agent harness model-agnostic. My LangGraph setup swaps models behind a protocol boundary without touching the graph. Two-week release cycles are precisely why that abstraction earns its keep.
  • Design for multi-day runs now. Checkpointing, resumable state, budget caps, and human-approval gates are the workloads Carbon is built for. If your agents can't survive a restart, they can't use this class of model well.
  • Price your workloads against cached input. My RAG re-ranking and re-scoring pipelines re-send enormous context on every call. At 95% off, the moment this API lands, those get radically cheaper — worth knowing before you architect around it.
  • Add security evals to your test suite. Find-a-bug, validate-the-exploit, patch-without-breaking-scope tasks. Whatever model you run, you want numbers on how it behaves under security pressure before production does.
  • If you do security work, watch the Fairwind rollout and get in early. This is the first frontier model where defenders hold the first-mover advantage — that window is the experiment.
  • Don't rip anything up for a benchmark crown. In the coding work most of us are paid for, Opus 5.5 still leads. Switch on your evals, not on headlines.

The benchmark race produces a new winner every few weeks now. What doesn't reset is architecture. The teams that win the next year won't be the ones that picked the right model in October — they'll be the ones whose systems let them pick any model, any week, without a rewrite. Carbon made that lesson more urgent, and it isn't even generally available yet.
