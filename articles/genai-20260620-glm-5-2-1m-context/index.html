GLM-5.2: The 744B Open-Weight Model With a 1M Context — and Why It Matters for Builders

June 20, 2026 · Open-Source LLMs / AI Models / Self-Hosting

On June 13, 2026, Z.ai (Zhipu) shipped GLM-5.2 — a 744-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with a genuine 1-million-token context window and MIT-licensed open weights. It is the strongest open-source coding model available right now, and its Terminal-Bench score lands within a few points of Claude Opus 4.8. I'm writing this article on top of GLM-5.2, so this is partly a first-hand account.

GLM-5.2 at a glance, plus the lineage from GLM-4.5 to today.

What makes GLM-5.2 worth your attention is not any single number. It is the combination: frontier-class coding ability, a context window long enough to swallow an entire repository, genuinely open weights you can download and keep — and it arrived on the exact day a top closed model went dark under export controls. That timing is not a footnote; it is the story.

Let's break down what actually shipped, how the architecture keeps a 744B model affordable, what the benchmarks really say, and the honest math of running it yourself.

What shipped: the spec card

GLM-5.2 is the third major iteration in the GLM-5 line, and the biggest jump yet. The headline specs:

  • 744B total parameters, ~40B active. A sparse Mixture-of-Experts design — the same active footprint as GLM-5.1, but the total knowledge surface is enormous.
  • 1,000,000-token context window (served as glm-5.2[1m]), with up to 131,072 output tokens per response. That is roughly five times larger than GLM-5.1's 200K window.
  • MIT-licensed open weights, rolling out to Hugging Face on a staggered schedule after launch.
  • Coding-first training focus with multiple thinking-effort levels (a "Max" reasoning mode) to balance performance against latency.

The non-obvious detail: the total parameter count did not budge from GLM-5.1. The win is entirely in context length, coding quality, and efficiency — not raw scale.

The lineage: scale, then specialize

Reading the GLM-5 history tells you where the budget went:

  • GLM-4.5 — 355B total, 32B active, 23T pre-training tokens.
  • GLM-5 (Feb 2026) — scaled hard to 744B / 40B active, 28.5T tokens, and integrated DeepSeek Sparse Attention to cut long-context cost.
  • GLM-5.1 — 200K context; the focus shifted to agentic engineering and staying productive over long horizons instead of exhausting its bag of tricks early.
  • GLM-5.2 (Jun 2026) — same 40B-active body, now with a stable 1M context and a coding-first post-training pass.

The pattern is clear: Z.ai spent GLM-5 buying scale, GLM-5.1 buying stamina, and GLM-5.2 buying context and precision. Each release kept the per-token cost roughly flat while expanding what the model can do.

Inside the MoE: making 744B affordable

How a 744B model avoids paying 744B-worth of FLOPs per token.

The reason a 744B model can be cheap to run is Mixture-of-Experts: a router sends each token to only a handful of the hundreds of available experts, so only ~40B of the 744B parameters actually fire per token. You get frontier-class knowledge with a dense-40B-class bill.

GLM-5.2 adds two engineering tricks on top, and both matter for anyone building at the 1M-context edge:

  • IndexShare. A new technique that reuses the same indexer across every four sparse-attention layers. Z.ai reports this cuts per-token FLOPs by 2.9× at a 1M context length. Long context is normally where attention cost explodes — IndexShare is the reason a million tokens is practical rather than a benchmark stunt.
  • An improved MTP (multi-token-prediction) layer for speculative decoding, which increases draft acceptance length by up to 20%. In practice: noticeably faster token generation, which compounds enormously when you're streaming a 131K-token output.

Net effect: a 744B model whose per-token and long-context cost sit closer to a dense 40B model than to its own total parameter count.

The benchmarks: closing the gap to the closed frontier

Vendor-reported coding scores. GLM-5.2 is the strongest open-source model.

Z.ai's own numbers are striking, and the improvement over GLM-5.1 is large enough to be the real story:

  • Terminal-Bench 2.1: 81.0, up from GLM-5.1's 62.0 — a 19-point jump on real-world terminal tasks.
  • SWE-bench Pro: 62.1, up from 58.4 — resolving actual GitHub-style software issues.
  • On Terminal-Bench, GLM-5.2 lands within ~4 points of Claude Opus 4.8 (85.0) and stays ahead of Gemini 3.1 Pro.

A calibration note: these are vendor-reported scores, and Gemini 3.1 Pro's exact figure isn't published — only the relative claim that GLM-5.2 leads it. Treat the absolute numbers as directional, not gospel. But the trend is unambiguous: the open-source coding frontier is no longer a full generation behind the closed one. The gap that used to be a canyon is now a few benchmark points.

The same day, two futures

June 13, 2026: one model went dark, another shipped its weights.

Here is the part that elevates GLM-5.2 from "interesting release" to "strategically important." On the same day it launched, Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 went offline globally under a US export-control directive — a model that reportedly hit ~80% on SWE-Bench Pro vanished from everyone overnight.

That contrast crystallizes a structural split in AI right now:

Closed frontier — access is a permission

  • API-only. No weights, ever.
  • Revocable: a provider (or a government) can pull access at any time.
  • Usage caps, rate limits, and geographic blocks you don't control.
  • Pricing set unilaterally with no cost floor.
  • You cannot audit, inspect, or fine-tune the core model.

Open weights — access is a download

  • Weights on Hugging Face, MIT-licensed, yours to keep.
  • Cannot be revoked once you have a copy.
  • No caps — your GPUs define your limits.
  • Cost floors to electricity plus hardware depreciation.
  • Full audit and fine-tune freedom, including air-gapped deployment.

If your product roadmap depends entirely on a single closed API, it is one policy decision away from a blackout. An open-weight baseline is how you buy resilience.

I'm not arguing open weights are always better — for many workloads the closed APIs are still smarter, cheaper to start, and less operational headache. But relying on only them is now a measurable strategic risk. The mature architecture is multi-model, with an open-weight fallback for any closed dependency you can't afford to lose.

The honest self-hosting math

744B is not a laptop workload. Here's what it actually takes.

MIT weights are a promise, not a deployment. "744B parameters" is a serious footprint, and the 1M context makes it harder, not easier. The memory budget breaks down roughly like this:

  • ~744 GB for FP8 weights alone — unavoidable.
  • A large KV cache for 1M-token contexts — this is the real constraint, and it grows with your batch size.
  • Runtime and activation overhead on top of both.

So you need comfortably more than 744 GB of combined VRAM. In practice that points at clusters like:

  • 8× H100 80GB (640 GB total) — too tight once you add KV cache for long context.
  • 8× H200 141GB (1,128 GB total) — comfortable headroom; the realistic sweet spot for full 1M context.
  • ~10× H100 or 5× B200-class — 800 GB+, enough for multi-batch long-context serving.

You serve it with vLLM or SGLang, and quantization (FP8 or INT4) materially lowers the floor. A rough deployment sketch looks like:

# Conceptual — adjust to your hardware / quant
vllm serve zai-org/glm-5.2 \
  --tensor-parallel-size 8 \
  --max-model-len 1000000 \
  --quantization fp8 \
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.92

When does self-hosting beat the API? Roughly: sustained, predictable volume, strict data sovereignty, an existing GPU cluster, or a fine-tuning requirement. For sporadic or bursty workloads, the Z.ai API is almost always cheaper and far less operational pain.

What this means for builders

Stepping back, GLM-5.2 is a useful forcing function for how you think about your model stack. Three takeaways:

  • Treat open weights as infrastructure, not ideology. A 744B MIT model with a 1M context and near-frontier coding is now a legitimate default, not a fallback you hope you never need. Design your routing so it can carry real load.
  • The 1M context changes architectures. Whole-repo reasoning, multi-file refactors, and long-horizon agent loops stop being hacky RAG workarounds. You can finally hand the model the entire context and let it reason end-to-end — but budget for the KV cache that implies.
  • Diversify before you're forced to. The Claude Fable 5 export ban is a live reminder that capability you don't own is capability you can lose. A multi-model setup with an open-weight baseline converts "catastrophic dependency" into "managed tradeoff."

Try it

The lowest-friction path is the Z.ai API and playground — you get the full 1M context with zero GPU ops. If you want to own it, watch the zai-org/GLM-5 repo for the MIT weight release and size your cluster to the math above.

GLM-5.2 won't win every benchmark, and the closed frontier will move again next week. But the days when "open source" meant a visible performance tax are over. The question for builders is no longer whether open weights are good enough — it's whether you've architected your stack to use them when it counts.
